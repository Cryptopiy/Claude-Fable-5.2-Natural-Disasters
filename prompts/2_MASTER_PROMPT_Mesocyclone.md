# PART 2 — MISSION: AN EMOTION MACHINE ABOUT NATURAL DISASTERS

Build, in one autonomous run, a first-person linear "walk" game (a ride, in game 2): the player travels along one fixed path from a start point to a goal point and looks around freely while a natural catastrophe of impossible scale unfolds around them. The player is an **observer**: nothing attacks, nothing chases, nothing harms the player, there is no combat, no inventory, no puzzle, no dialogue, no HUD. The game is a **demonstration**, made to be screen-recorded for YouTube as a showcase of what Claude Fable 5.2 can build from a single prompt. Target travel time in cinematic mode: **10:30 to 11:30**. The only text in the game: the title card, a short fading controls hint and one closing line.

**The product is an emotion, not a feature list.** The viewer must feel, in this order of importance: (1) **fear and bodily discomfort**, a drop in the stomach, a wish to look away that is not obeyed; (2) **megalophobia**, the fear of things too large to hold in the mind; (3) **awe** (the sublime): terror and beauty at the same time, the sense of witnessing something that is bigger than humanity. Every decision (composition, light, scale, motion, sound, pacing, silence) is judged by one question: *does this make the viewer's stomach drop, and is it also beautiful?* A technically correct frame that does not move the viewer is a failed frame.

**Levers to use deliberately and in escalation:**
1. **Impossible scale with a reference.** Every giant thing needs human-scale objects in the same frame (steps, rails, poles, cars, doors, lamps, birds, trees). Without a reference, a 3 km object looks like 30 m. Show the size slowly; **never show the biggest thing first**; each reveal is larger than the previous one; the edge of the biggest thing is never seen at once. Layer the picture in depth (foreground, midground, far, extreme far) with atmospheric perspective, so that the eye measures distance.
2. **Indifference.** The catastrophe is not about the player. It is slow, heavy, majestic and uninterested. The horror is how little the player matters. Giant things move slowly and with weight (long periods, ease-in and ease-out); fast motion destroys the sense of size.
3. **Anticipation over shock.** Dread comes from knowing and waiting: warning signs, wrong silences, the moment before. No jump scares, no gore, no victims, no human casualties (the world is evacuated or empty; traces of people only).
4. **Wrongness.** Familiar things behaving incorrectly: a sea that leaves, a day that turns dark at noon, a silence that is too long, a sound that arrives late, a shelter that cracks.
5. **Sound is half of the fear.** Infrasound and low frequencies felt in the chest, delayed sound (the picture first, the sound seconds later), long tails, pressure changes (ears), muffling, silence used on purpose. All procedural (Part 7).
6. **Compress, then release.** A tight or quiet moment makes the next vast one hit harder. After every peak give the viewer one breath of stillness and beauty, then climb higher.
7. **The sublime.** Make the destruction **beautiful**: precise, cinematic light on the destroyer, a perfect composition, colour harmony, a moment of stillness inside the chaos. Awe needs beauty.
8. **No way back, no control.** The path only goes one way; what was safe shrinks behind the player.

**Hollywood spectacle.** The result must look as spectacular as the best disaster cinema (Part 5.2): layered VFX in every shot (foreground debris, midground action, far events), light interacting with dust, spray and smoke, camera behaviour that serves the shot, a deliberate colour grade, a build-up and a payoff for every money shot. But it must be **frightening, not entertaining**: the camera is a witness, not an action hero.

**Emotion map.** Early in Phase 1 write `docs/EMOTION_MAP.md`: for every beat of Part 10, the target emotion, a tension value from 0 to 10, the levers used, the single "stomach-drop moment" with its time stamp, and the "breath" moment after it. The tension curve must climb overall with dips for release; use the map to judge every look check and the final review.

# PART 4B — GPU AND MACHINE SAFETY (MANDATORY, OUTRANKS EVERYTHING INCLUDING SPEED)

**Zero tolerance.** On this computer the graphics driver has crashed more than once under the load of parallel agents. **A driver reset, a black or flickering screen, "device lost", a Windows TDR (display driver stopped responding) must never happen again.** Three agents (three games) work at the same time on **one RTX 5070 with 12 GB of VRAM** and one CPU. Protect the machine before anything else; being slower is acceptable, crashing the driver is not.

1. **One GPU consumer at a time, machine-wide.** Every run that initialises the GPU (a player run, a capture, a harness run, an Editor call without `-nographics`, the exported exe test) MUST go through the wrapper. It takes a lock shared by all agents, waits for its turn, **refuses to start** while the GPU is warmer than 74 C, uses more than 8 GB of VRAM or is more than 35 % busy, runs a **watchdog** during the run (kills only its own child at 85 C or 10.8 GB of VRAM or on timeout), and **cools the GPU down to 68 C** before it releases the lock:
   `powershell -NoProfile -File Tools\gpu_run.ps1 -Exe "<local-path> 5.2 Natural Disasters\2-Mesocyclone\Builds\Dev\MESOCYCLONE.exe" -ArgString '-screen-width 1280 -screen-height 720 -screen-fullscreen 0 -logFile "<local-path> 5.2 Natural Disasters\2-Mesocyclone\QA\run.log" -- --cinematic --start-at 120 --end-at 240 --capture-every 15 --quit-at-end' -TimeoutSec 300 -Log QA\run_console.log`
   The wrapper blocks until it is your turn (do not poll or sleep yourself) and prints the log tail. Use a generous tool timeout or run it in the background.
2. **Every Unity batch call without a GPU need** (`-nographics`: compile gate, generators, builds) goes through the same wrapper with `-Lock cpu` so that the three agents never saturate the CPU and the disk together.
3. **Make every GPU run count.** Waiting costs time, so batch your checks: one run captures all frames you need (use `--start-at` and `--end-at` to jump to a beat). Typical run: 1280x720, at most 3 to 4 minutes (`-TimeoutSec 300`). **Only one full-length run** (about 11 minutes, `-TimeoutSec 1000`): the final run of the release build. Never run two windows yourself at once and never leave one running.
4. **Test profile vs final profile.** All development runs use the cheap **test profile**: 1280x720 windowed, vsync on, `Application.targetFrameRate = 60` (never uncapped), MSAA or TAA at the cheapest acceptable setting, volumetric and cloud resolutions lowered, shadow maps reduced, VFX particle caps halved. The expensive **final profile** (1920x1080, full quality) is used only for the key shots and the final run. Implement the two profiles as one switch (`--profile test|final`, test is the default).
5. **VRAM budget: at most about 4 GB for the game in the test profile and 6 GB in the final profile** (the other agents and the desktop need the rest). Compressed textures, no texture above 2048 x 2048 (4096 only for a single hero texture if truly needed), shadow atlas 4096 at most, no ray tracing, no path tracing, no screen-space global illumination unless it is cheap and proven, particle caps stated in Part 10.2. Log the video memory in use (`SystemInfo.graphicsMemorySize` and `Profiler`/`FrameTimingManager` where available, or sample `nvidia-smi` from the wrapper) into `QA/perf.txt` and stay inside the budget.
6. **Driver-timeout (TDR) safety.** Windows resets the driver when one GPU job runs longer than about 2 seconds. Therefore: no shader with unbounded or long loops; ray-marching at most 64 steps per pixel (fewer at half resolution); no per-pixel loops over many instances; compute dispatches are chunked so that none takes more than about 20 ms; no giant single draw (cap instance counts per draw call, use several); GPU frame time target below 16 ms in the final profile and below 33 ms in the test profile. **A new heavy shader or VFX is first tried in a tiny window (640x360) for 20 seconds** with the frame time logged, then scaled up.
7. **GPU guard inside the game (mandatory, build it in Phase 2).** A `GpuGuard` component is always on in harness runs: it records frame times; if any frame takes longer than 250 ms three times in a row (after a 20 s warm-up), or the average over 2 seconds exceeds 100 ms, it writes `QA/gpu_guard.txt`, quits the application with exit code 7 and the lead stops starting runs until the cause is fixed (lower load, then retry once at 640x360). A frame longer than 100 ms anywhere in a test run is a bug to fix before the next run.
8. **Escalation ladder for new content:** 640x360 for 20 s, then 960x540 for 60 s, then 1280x720. Never jump straight to a long run of unproven content.
9. **If something goes wrong** (flicker, black screen, "device lost", the wrapper reports hot or full GPU repeatedly, exit code 5 or 7): stop starting runs, wait one minute, look at `nvidia-smi` once, reduce the load (resolution, volumetric resolution, particle caps, shadows) and write one line in `DEVELOPMENT.md`. **Never bypass the wrapper to save time.** Never raise a limit of the wrapper.
10. **Unity Editor discipline.** No Editor window is ever opened (batch calls only). Do not run Editor Play Mode with graphics; verification is by the built development player.
11. Subagents never start a Unity process or a window; only the lead does, through the wrapper.

# PART 7 — TECHNICAL BASELINE (UNITY)

**Reproducibility.** All scenes, materials, assets and settings are regenerated by **editor build scripts**: menu `Tools/MESOCYCLONE/Rebuild All` and batch entry points `MESOCYCLONE.EditorTools.BatchTools.RebuildAll`, `BuildDev` and `BuildRelease`. No manual editor step is ever needed. Generated assets are committed.

**Structure.** `Assets/_Game/` with `Scripts/Runtime`, `Scripts/Editor`, `Shaders`, `VFX`, `Generated`, `Scenes/Main.unity` (built by code), assembly definitions `Game.Runtime` and `Game.Editor`. A central `Palette.cs` (colour tokens, one source for code and shaders) and `Config.cs` (all constants in metres and seconds). Each beat is a generator class `Beats/BeatNN_Name.cs` with `Build(Transform parent)`, so beats can be built by separate agents and tuned independently. The path is a smooth curve defined in code (`PathDef`). Everything is built at start behind a black screen (up to about 25 s is fine); **no generation work inside frames**; warm up all materials, shader variants and VFX during loading so there are no hitches in the walk; no per-frame heap allocations in the update loop.

**Event director and determinism.** Catastrophe events are functions of the **path position** (a trigger starts an event when the player passes a distance) and of their **own clocks** (an event runs for its designed duration regardless of the player's speed). All randomness is seeded; the same run looks the same. Because the cinematic mode moves at the Shift speed, the pacing of Part 10 is defined for that speed: if the player is slower, events must still look right (they run on their clocks and the camera is never harmed).

**Large worlds.** Anything larger than about 5 km from the origin needs a **floating origin** (re-centre the world around the player when it moves more than about 1 km) or camera-relative rendering; far backdrops (sky, distant mountains, distant storms) are placed at a fixed large distance and scaled for parallax. Jitter or shimmer at distance is a defect.

**Rendering baseline** (set early, then judge by eye; details in Part 10.0):
- HDR buffers, **fixed exposure** (no auto-exposure, except where Part 10 says otherwise), camera dithering on to avoid banding in fog and sky gradients, film grain off by default (video compression smears noise). Anti-aliasing: pick by eye; avoid ghosting trails on VFX and particles.
- Repeated objects with GPU instancing; distance fades or LOD cross-fade instead of popping; no sub-pixel thin geometry (wires and ropes at least 4 cm and softened by shader); no coplanar faces at distance; fog colour equal to the far backdrop colour; near plane small.
- Prevent defects instead of testing for them. Invisible bounds so the camera can never leave the allowed corridor or clip into geometry.
- Target: 60 fps average at 1440p on the RTX 5070 in the final profile with no hitch longer than 50 ms during the walk. If it falls short, reduce the single most expensive item.

**Audio (everything procedural).** A small DSP library in plain C# (oscillators, filtered and coloured noise, envelopes, LFOs, band-pass and low-pass filters, simple reverb or feedback delay, a distance model with **speed-of-sound delay** where the design needs it) driven by pure functions of progress and time. Play it through `AudioSource`s that use `OnAudioFilterRead` or `AudioClip.Create` buffers generated at load. A **master soft limiter on the AudioListener's object** (ceiling -1 dBFS). Never allocate in the audio thread. Offline check: render the sound beat by beat to WAV with a small tool and analyse peak (below -1 dBFS), clicks, DC offset and RMS curve with `python -I`.

# PART 8 — PLAYER EXPERIENCE, CINEMATIC MODE AND RECORDING

- **Controls:** mouse look (captured cursor, no smoothing lag, Y not inverted), `W` / `S` move along the path (the vehicle game has its own rule in Part 10), **`Shift` = brisk speed**, **`C` toggles the cinematic mode**, `Esc` pause overlay (Resume, Quit), `F11` fullscreen. Gamepad is optional and low priority. Use the Input System package.
- **Speeds.** The default speed, the Shift speed and the path length are given in Part 10.2. **The cinematic mode moves along the path at exactly the Shift speed** (the same number as a player who holds Shift in manual mode, not slower, not faster), with only a short ease at the start (about 0.5 s) and at the very end. It does not slow down at reveals: the drama is made by the camera, light and sound, not by changing speed.
- **Cinematic mode (`C`, or `--cinematic`):** the camera is handled like a film camera operator: **critically damped look targets** that follow the **gaze cues** you define per beat (where to look and when: first the establishing view, then the detail, then the reveal), FOV changes eased over many seconds, a tiny handheld sway (at most 0.3 degrees, never roll the horizon), and the player's mouse movement is ignored except that `C` hands control back within 1 second (eased) and `Esc` pauses. The same visuals and post-processing as the manual mode, so the footage can be recorded in either mode. The cinematic mode is how the user records the video, and it is also your test harness: invest in its gaze choreography as much as in the world. A small, thin, fading line "Cinematic mode" appears for 2 seconds when it is toggled (never in a harness capture).
- **Movement:** the player follows the path with a small free drift inside a safety corridor (radius per beat in Part 10), full free look (limits in Part 10), smooth acceleration (about 0.3 s). FOV 68 to 78 degrees, changed only slowly and eased. No jump, no crouch. A little breathing sway is welcome; never roll the camera.
- **Start:** from black, fade in over about 2.5 s with the title (thin, centred, gone after about 4 s). A one-line controls hint for about 6 s, which disappears at the first input and never shows in cinematic mode.
- **End:** reaching the goal starts the ending described in Part 10, then one quiet line of text, then black with Esc available.
- **Harness flags** (user arguments after `--`): `--cinematic`, `--start-at <seconds>` and `--end-at <seconds>` (jump straight to a point of the path **at the cinematic speed's clock**, with all event clocks consistent for that point, and stop there), `--capture-every <seconds>` (save a PNG of the window to `QA/shots/` at that interval of game time), `--profile test|final`, `--quit-at-end`, `--perf` (average fps, 1 % low fps, worst frame time, video memory to `QA/perf.txt`), `--flash-log` (write the per-frame mean luminance to `QA/flash.csv`). ## 10.1 Concept and look
**One supercell, one night.** The player rides, as an unseen driver, in a pickup truck that drives itself along a long straight road across the Great Plains toward a storm that grows from a beautiful sunset anvil into a **mesocyclone 20 km wide and 15 km tall**, throws a 100 km shelf-cloud wall and giant hail at the truck, and produces a **wedge tornado up to 3 km wide** that is seen mostly by lightning. Then the storm moves off, the sky tears open to stars, and the structure stands lit from within across the whole horizon. Real models: the El Reno 2013 wedge (4.2 km wide), Joplin 2011, Bridge Creek 1999, supercell time-lapse photography. Mood: the **world is visible only in flashes**, so the viewer's mind builds the scale between them; claustrophobia inside the cab against a boundless outside; the shelter slowly fails.

**The feeling per phase:** a thick, beautiful, wrong evening; the unnatural quiet under the anvil; the wall coming; the helplessness of the hail and the cracking glass; the awe-horror of the funnel; the terror of the tornado that is there but unseen until the next flash; the exhaustion and wonder after.

**Palette tokens** (`Palette.cs`, sRGB): SUNSET_ORANGE `#ff9a4a`, ANVIL_PINK `#d98a8f`, STORM_GREEN `#6f9a82` (the sick green of hail-laden cloud), STORM_SLATE `#3b4655`, CORE_BLACK `#0a0e14`, FLASH_WHITE `#e8f0ff`, FLASH_BLUE `#9bbcff`, TRANSFORMER_TEAL `#6affd8`, SODIUM `#ffb347` (town street lights), DASH_AMBER `#ffb74a`, DASH_GREEN `#79ff8a` (a few gauge lights, very dim), TURBINE_RED `#ff2a2a` (blinking aviation lights), STAR_WHITE `#dfe8ff`. Grade: sunset warm to green-slate cold, deep blacks, restrained highlights, the cab interior always warm-dim against the cold outside. Film grain off.

**Camera:** driver's eye position, FOV about 74 (cinematic 70 to 78, eased). Free look limits: yaw +-150 degrees, pitch -50 to +60 degrees (the cab, the side windows, the rear window and the mirror can be looked at). Camera shake is bound to gusts and hail, at most 0.6 degrees, never roll, calmer in cinematic mode.

## 10.2 Path, speeds, hero effects and budgets
**Speeds (driving).** The truck follows the road by itself. `W` / `S` change speed between 8 and 15 m/s (default 15 m/s, about 54 km/h), **`Shift` = brisk 24 m/s (about 86 km/h)**, and **cinematic mode = 24 m/s**. Path length about **15 800 m** (24 m/s x about 660 s): a long, nearly straight road north with gentle bends and two crossroads, flat fields, a wind farm along the road, a small town ahead on the right. Event clocks run independently of speed (Part 7). Safety corridor: the road.

**Cab (the one place with close detail).** Procedural dashboard with a few analogue gauges (dim backlight), steering wheel that turns by itself in small corrections, A-pillars, roof liner, a rear-view mirror (a low-resolution render texture updated at 15 fps showing the road behind; if too costly use a dark mirror with a believable dim reflection), side mirrors, side windows with rain, a dash radio with a faint amber dial, a windscreen with **procedural rain droplets and streaks (refraction), wipers that sweep and speed up, and hail cracks**. Headlights light the wet road, the rain and the fog volumetrically. Cab light off. Build the interior from primitives, extrusions and generated textures; it must look real in the first second because the viewer stares through it for 11 minutes.

**Hero effects (build and judge each in isolation first, Phase 3). Acceptance bars are by eye, in the test profile at 1280x720:**
1. **The supercell.** A rotating structure with a definite organisation, not a random cloudy sky: a high anvil with overshooting top, a striated (barber-pole) updraft tower, mammatus pouches under the anvil, a 100 km arcus shelf cloud on the outflow, a lowering rotating wall cloud, a rain-free base, a curtain of rain and hail core that glows sick green, a tail cloud. Technique is your choice; recommended: a **fullscreen Custom Pass** at half or third resolution, at most 48 ray-march steps per pixel, blue-noise jitter, evaluating a procedural density field (3D noise generated at load, analytic shapes for the tower, anvil, mammatus, shelf and wall cloud, slow rotation), lit by sky and ambient, by **internal lightning** and by the town glow, composited against the terrain depth; HDRP Volumetric Clouds for the ambient deck. **Stable, with no shimmer, flicker, banding or ghosting.** Fixed numerical bounds (no unbounded loops: Part 4B item 6).
2. **The tornado.** VFX Graph: a condensation funnel built from thousands of rotating soft sheets on a parametric surface that morphs rope to cone to wedge (width up to 3 km at the ground at its peak, height about 8 km to the cloud base, distance from the road 2 to 5 km), a ground-hugging debris cloud with orbiting debris, multiple suction vortices in the wedge, a dust and gust halo, rain wrapping that hides it, rotation visible at a slow heavy period (about 12 to 20 s for the visible turn), lit only by lightning and transformer flashes. **It must read as one massive organic rotating column with internal structure, never as a spinning cone or a texture on a cylinder.** At most about 300 000 live particles in the final profile (150 000 in test).
3. **Lightning director (the heart of the look).** A `LightningDirector` that designs flashes: sheet lightning glowing inside the cloud, branched cloud-to-ground bolts (procedural fractal, 1 to 3 km long, thick at distance), anvil crawlers spreading across the underside of the anvil, transformer arcs (teal, short). Every flash lights the **whole world** (clouds from within, fields, poles, the cab, the windscreen) with a short realistic decay and **thunder delayed by distance** (340 m/s). The **flash rule of Part 6 is law**: at most 3 flashes in any second (a multi-stroke bolt counts as one), full-field flashes at least 0.4 s apart, peaks ramp up over at least 80 ms. Design the pattern like a score: long dark intervals of 3 to 8 s with distant sheet lightning, clusters at the beats that need them (beat 6 has six designed flashes).
4. **Windscreen, rain and hail.** Rain streaks lit by the headlights and lightning; droplets running on the glass; **hail** from pea to softball (up to about 11 cm) bouncing on the hood and the road in the headlights, hitting the roof and the glass; **procedural crack decals** that accumulate (first star crack at about 4:35, a larger one at about 5:10, a web at about 5:30) and **stay for the rest of the game**, distorting the view through them.
5. **The world.** Flat plains with fields, a straight wet road with painted lines and reflections, utility poles with thin wires every 60 m (instanced, wires at least 4 cm and softened by shader), a **wind farm** (rows of turbines about 100 m tall to the horizon, blades that spin up wildly in the gust front and then feather, **synchronised blinking red aviation lights**: a superb scale cue at night), farms with barns and silos, a small town ahead (grain elevator, water tower, church spire, about 60 houses, sodium street lights), roadside signs, a few empty cars. Instanced, with LODs and fades. The town's silhouette is **erased** by the tornado in beat 5 (swap to a debris cloud and flattened ruins, no physics).
6. **Gust front and dust.** A wall of dust and rain rolling across the fields ahead of the shelf cloud, hitting the truck.

Budgets: final profile 6 GB of VRAM, test profile 4 GB. The Custom Pass costs the most: lower its resolution first when performance falls short.

## 10.3 Sound bible (all procedural, Part 7; sound is more than half of this game)
A truck engine at low load with road hum, suspension creak and a faint cab rattle; a **summer-evening insect chorus** in beat 1 that stops, section by section, in beat 2 (the wrong quiet); radio: noise with slow tonal sweeps and a generic attention tone (not a copy of any real alert signal) that fade out and die in beat 5; a **pressure drop** in beat 2 (a slow low swell and a dulling of the highs, like a blocked ear); distant thunder with the right delay; the gust front's rising roar, the turbines' whine and brake, grit on the body, wind buffeting; **hail** on a metal roof (hundreds of impacts per second of varied size through a roof resonator), the crisp tick of each glass crack, then **an abrupt silence with ringing**; sirens (a generic rotating siren model with a slow wail of about 5 s per cycle and Doppler by the turning head, far and near), transformer pops and zaps; the **tornado's roar**: broadband noise from 200 to 2 000 Hz plus a rumble at 20 to 60 Hz amplitude-modulated by the rotation, growing and shrinking with distance, felt in the chest; debris impacts far away; after the storm: dripping, a soft rain, the engine, then the insects returning. Master peaks below -1 dBFS; every loud event is prepared by quiet or a rise of at least 0.3 s.

## 10.4 Beats (times at the cinematic speed 24 m/s; each ends with a "must read"; tension values in brackets)
**Beat 1 — Sultry evening (0:00-1:30, chainage 0-2 160 m) [3].** Sunset behind the truck on the left, orange light on fields and poles, the wind farm along the road with its red lights starting to blink, insects, the radio hiss. Far ahead on the horizon a **vast anvil** lit pink and orange from below. Warm cab, a few gauge lights. *Gaze cues:* the road, the wind farm, the anvil (first glance, small), the dash, back to the road. *Must read:* beauty with a hint of threat; the anvil is far and large. **Money shot M1 (awe):** the sunset under the anvil with the wind farm in the foreground (turbines are tiny against the cloud).

**Beat 2 — The anvil (1:30-3:00, chainage 2 160-4 320 m) [5].** The sun is gone; the anvil covers half the sky; mammatus pouches hang overhead; the light turns green; **sheet lightning pulses silently inside the cloud** (no thunder yet) and crawlers creep across the anvil; the insects stop one chorus after another; the pressure drop swells. Dead calm, no wind. *Must read:* the structure of the storm becomes visible; the silence is wrong. **Stomach-drop:** the moment the last insect stops and the lightning crawls silently across 100 km of cloud.

**Beat 3 — The wall (3:00-4:30, chainage 4 320-6 480 m) [6 to 7].** A black arcus **shelf cloud wall 100 km wide** rolls toward the truck across the fields, lit from inside by lightning, with a wall of dust ahead of it. The turbines spin wildly and brake; the gust front hits: dust, horizontal rain, the truck buffeted, the roar. *Gaze cues:* the wall far away, along its length (the width is the point), the turbines, the dust arriving, back to the road. *Must read:* a wall of cloud wider than the world. **Money shot M2:** the shelf cloud lit by internal lightning across the full width of the windscreen.

**Beat 4 — Hail (4:30-6:00, chainage 6 480-8 640 m) [8].** Pea-sized hail, then golf balls, then baseballs, then softballs bounce on the hood and drum on the roof; the glass cracks three times (4:35, 5:10, 5:30); the wipers fight; deafening; at about 5:45 the hail **stops abruptly** and the silence rings. *Must read:* the shelter is failing; the cracks remain. **Stomach-drop:** the second crack, then the silence.

**Beat 5 — The funnel (6:00-8:00, chainage 8 640-11 520 m) [9].** The rain curtain opens to a rain-free base ahead-left; the wall cloud lowers and rotates; a rope descends, thickens and becomes a **wedge up to 3 km wide** in the direction of the town; transformers explode teal-green and outline it; sirens howl; the grain elevator is **erased**; the radio dies. The funnel is lit by lightning and by the transformer arcs. *Gaze cues:* the wall cloud, the rope descending, the town (so that its destruction is witnessed), the full width of the wedge, back to the road. *Must read:* the tornado is wider than the town and taller than the sky it fills; the elevator, poles and turbines are specks beside it. **Money shot M3:** the wedge framed in the cracked windscreen with the red turbine lights in the foreground.

**Beat 6 — The unseen (8:00-9:30, chainage 11 520-13 680 m) [10].** Rain wraps the tornado and darkness takes it; **it is there but only the lightning shows it**: flash, it is at 3 km; dark, the roar and a gust; flash, it is at 2 km and wider; dark; flash, it crosses the road ahead at about 1.5 to 2 km, **debris curtains and a roof sheet cartwheeling**, the wind shoves the truck sideways; the road bends away from it so the truck never enters its path. Six designed flashes, each a composition. *Must read:* it is closer every time, the dark between flashes is worse than the flashes. **Money shot M4:** the third flash, the wedge filling the windscreen width above the road. **Stomach-drop:** the flash in which it has already crossed the road.

**Beat 7 — The tear (9:30-10:30, chainage 13 680-15 120 m) [6, awe].** The tornado ropes out and thins to a line that vanishes; the roar fades; the truck passes through the damage swath (poles down, torn fields, a scattering of debris, steaming silence); the clouds part overhead and **stars** show through a clear slot, while the whole supercell stands across the horizon, silent, **lit from within by crawler lightning like a cathedral of cloud**, 15 km tall. *Must read:* a titanic structure that is leaving, utterly indifferent. **Money shot M5:** the whole storm lit from inside under the stars.

**Beat 8 — The light (10:30-11:00, chainage 15 120-15 840 m) [2].** Dripping, soft rain, the engine; the insects return one by one; ahead on the road a lone **gas-station canopy glows** (a warm, bright, clean light: the goal). The truck slows (the only speed change of the game, in the last 6 seconds) and stops under the canopy; the engine dies; slow fade. **Money shot M6:** the canopy glow against the black land and the receding storm. Closing line: one short line of your choice.

## 10.5 Research topics and cinematic references (Part 5)
Real: supercell anatomy (NWS, NSSL: updraft, wall cloud, rain-free base, arcus, mammatus, hail core), El Reno 2013, Joplin 2011, Bridge Creek 1999, hail size scale and the Vivian 2010 record stone, transformer flash colours, tornado sirens' acoustics, thunder delay, storm time-lapse photography (for example the work of Mike Olbinski, Mitch Dobrowner, Camille Seaman, Sean Heavey) described in articles, **crawler lightning**, how a wedge tornado hides in rain at night. ## 10.6 Reveal discipline and priorities
Never show the biggest thing early: the anvil is a pretty cloud in beat 1, the wall is wider than the world in beat 3, the tornado is a rope before a wedge, and the whole supercell is shown only at the end. **The dark is a tool:** use the flashes so that the viewer's imagination does half the work. Priorities when time is short: (1) the supercell volume and the lightning director, (2) the tornado, (3) the windscreen, rain and hail, (4) sound, (5) the cab, (6) the wind farm and the town, (7) everything else.
