# PART 2 — MISSION: AN EMOTION MACHINE ABOUT NATURAL DISASTERS

Build, in one autonomous run, a first-person linear "walk" game (a ride, in game 2): the player travels along one fixed path from a start point to a goal point and looks around freely while a natural catastrophe of impossible scale unfolds around them. The player is an **observer**: nothing attacks, nothing chases, nothing harms the player, there is no combat, no inventory, no puzzle, no dialogue, no HUD. The game is a **demonstration**, made to be screen-recorded for YouTube as a showcase of what Claude Fable 5.2 can build from a single prompt. Target travel time in cinematic mode: **10:30 to 11:30**. The only text in the game: the title card, a short fading controls hint and one closing line.

**The product is an emotion, not a feature list.** The viewer must feel, in this order of importance: (1) **fear and bodily discomfort**, a drop in the stomach, a wish to look away that is not obeyed; (2) **megalophobia**, the fear of things too large to hold in the mind; (3) **awe** (the sublime): terror and beauty at the same time, the sense of witnessing something that is bigger than humanity. Every decision (composition, light, scale, motion, sound, pacing, silence) is judged by one question: *does this make the viewer's stomach drop, and is it also beautiful?* A technically correct frame that does not move the viewer is a failed frame.

**Levers to use deliberately and in escalation:**
1. **Impossible scale with a reference.** Every giant thing needs human-scale objects in the same frame (steps, rails, poles, cars, doors, lamps, birds, trees). Without a reference, a 3 km object looks like 30 m. Show the size slowly; **never show the biggest thing first**; each reveal is larger than the previous one; the edge of the biggest thing is never seen at once. Layer the picture in depth (foreground, midground, far, extreme far) with atmospheric perspective, so that the eye measures distance.
2. **Indifference.** The catastrophe is not about the player. It is slow, heavy, majestic and uninterested. The horror is how little the player matters. Giant things move slowly and with weight (long periods, ease-in and ease-out); fast motion destroys the sense of size.
3. **Anticipation over shock.** Dread comes from knowing and waiting: warning signs, wrong silences, the moment before. No jump scares, no gore, no victims, no human casualties (the world is evacuated or empty; traces of people only).
4. **Wrongness.** Familiar things behaving incorrectly: a sea that leaves, a day that turns dark at noon, a silence that is too long, a sound that arrives late, a shelter that cracks.
5. **Sound is half of the fear.** Infrasound and low frequencies felt in the chest, delayed sound (the picture first, the sound seconds later), long tails, pressure changes (ears), muffling, silence used on purpose. All procedural (Part 7).
6. **Compress, then release.** A tight or quiet moment makes the next vast one hit harder. After every peak give the viewer one breath of stillness and beauty, then climb higher.
7. **The sublime.** Make the destruction **beautiful**: precise, cinematic light on the destroyer, a perfect composition, colour harmony, a moment of stillness inside the chaos. Awe needs beauty.
8. **No way back, no control.** The path only goes one way; what was safe shrinks behind the player.

**Hollywood spectacle.** The result must look as spectacular as the best disaster cinema (Part 5.2): layered VFX in every shot (foreground debris, midground action, far events), light interacting with dust, spray and smoke, camera behaviour that serves the shot, a deliberate colour grade, a build-up and a payoff for every money shot. But it must be **frightening, not entertaining**: the camera is a witness, not an action hero.

**Emotion map.** Early in Phase 1 write `docs/EMOTION_MAP.md`: for every beat of Part 10, the target emotion, a tension value from 0 to 10, the levers used, the single "stomach-drop moment" with its time stamp, and the "breath" moment after it. The tension curve must climb overall with dips for release; use the map to judge every look check and the final review.

# PART 4B — GPU AND MACHINE SAFETY (MANDATORY, OUTRANKS EVERYTHING INCLUDING SPEED)

**Zero tolerance.** On this computer the graphics driver has crashed more than once under the load of parallel agents. **A driver reset, a black or flickering screen, "device lost", a Windows TDR (display driver stopped responding) must never happen again.** Three agents (three games) work at the same time on **one RTX 5070 with 12 GB of VRAM** and one CPU. Protect the machine before anything else; being slower is acceptable, crashing the driver is not.

1. **One GPU consumer at a time, machine-wide.** Every run that initialises the GPU (a player run, a capture, a harness run, an Editor call without `-nographics`, the exported exe test) MUST go through the wrapper. It takes a lock shared by all agents, waits for its turn, **refuses to start** while the GPU is warmer than 74 C, uses more than 8 GB of VRAM or is more than 35 % busy, runs a **watchdog** during the run (kills only its own child at 85 C or 10.8 GB of VRAM or on timeout), and **cools the GPU down to 68 C** before it releases the lock:
   `powershell -NoProfile -File Tools\gpu_run.ps1 -Exe "<local-path> 5.2 Natural Disasters\3-Zero\Builds\Dev\ZERO.exe" -ArgString '-screen-width 1280 -screen-height 720 -screen-fullscreen 0 -logFile "<local-path> 5.2 Natural Disasters\3-Zero\QA\run.log" -- --cinematic --start-at 120 --end-at 240 --capture-every 15 --quit-at-end' -TimeoutSec 300 -Log QA\run_console.log`
   The wrapper blocks until it is your turn (do not poll or sleep yourself) and prints the log tail. Use a generous tool timeout or run it in the background.
2. **Every Unity batch call without a GPU need** (`-nographics`: compile gate, generators, builds) goes through the same wrapper with `-Lock cpu` so that the three agents never saturate the CPU and the disk together.
3. **Make every GPU run count.** Waiting costs time, so batch your checks: one run captures all frames you need (use `--start-at` and `--end-at` to jump to a beat). Typical run: 1280x720, at most 3 to 4 minutes (`-TimeoutSec 300`). **Only one full-length run** (about 11 minutes, `-TimeoutSec 1000`): the final run of the release build. Never run two windows yourself at once and never leave one running.
4. **Test profile vs final profile.** All development runs use the cheap **test profile**: 1280x720 windowed, vsync on, `Application.targetFrameRate = 60` (never uncapped), MSAA or TAA at the cheapest acceptable setting, volumetric and cloud resolutions lowered, shadow maps reduced, VFX particle caps halved. The expensive **final profile** (1920x1080, full quality) is used only for the key shots and the final run. Implement the two profiles as one switch (`--profile test|final`, test is the default).
5. **VRAM budget: at most about 4 GB for the game in the test profile and 6 GB in the final profile** (the other agents and the desktop need the rest). Compressed textures, no texture above 2048 x 2048 (4096 only for a single hero texture if truly needed), shadow atlas 4096 at most, no ray tracing, no path tracing, no screen-space global illumination unless it is cheap and proven, particle caps stated in Part 10.2. Log the video memory in use (`SystemInfo.graphicsMemorySize` and `Profiler`/`FrameTimingManager` where available, or sample `nvidia-smi` from the wrapper) into `QA/perf.txt` and stay inside the budget.
6. **Driver-timeout (TDR) safety.** Windows resets the driver when one GPU job runs longer than about 2 seconds. Therefore: no shader with unbounded or long loops; ray-marching at most 64 steps per pixel (fewer at half resolution); no per-pixel loops over many instances; compute dispatches are chunked so that none takes more than about 20 ms; no giant single draw (cap instance counts per draw call, use several); GPU frame time target below 16 ms in the final profile and below 33 ms in the test profile. **A new heavy shader or VFX is first tried in a tiny window (640x360) for 20 seconds** with the frame time logged, then scaled up.
7. **GPU guard inside the game (mandatory, build it in Phase 2).** A `GpuGuard` component is always on in harness runs: it records frame times; if any frame takes longer than 250 ms three times in a row (after a 20 s warm-up), or the average over 2 seconds exceeds 100 ms, it writes `QA/gpu_guard.txt`, quits the application with exit code 7 and the lead stops starting runs until the cause is fixed (lower load, then retry once at 640x360). A frame longer than 100 ms anywhere in a test run is a bug to fix before the next run.
8. **Escalation ladder for new content:** 640x360 for 20 s, then 960x540 for 60 s, then 1280x720. Never jump straight to a long run of unproven content.
9. **If something goes wrong** (flicker, black screen, "device lost", the wrapper reports hot or full GPU repeatedly, exit code 5 or 7): stop starting runs, wait one minute, look at `nvidia-smi` once, reduce the load (resolution, volumetric resolution, particle caps, shadows) and write one line in `DEVELOPMENT.md`. **Never bypass the wrapper to save time.** Never raise a limit of the wrapper.
10. **Unity Editor discipline.** No Editor window is ever opened (batch calls only). Do not run Editor Play Mode with graphics; verification is by the built development player.
11. Subagents never start a Unity process or a window; only the lead does, through the wrapper.

# PART 7 — TECHNICAL BASELINE (UNITY)

**Reproducibility.** All scenes, materials, assets and settings are regenerated by **editor build scripts**: menu `Tools/ZERO/Rebuild All` and batch entry points `ZERO.EditorTools.BatchTools.RebuildAll`, `BuildDev` and `BuildRelease`. No manual editor step is ever needed. Generated assets are committed.

**Structure.** `Assets/_Game/` with `Scripts/Runtime`, `Scripts/Editor`, `Shaders`, `VFX`, `Generated`, `Scenes/Main.unity` (built by code), assembly definitions `Game.Runtime` and `Game.Editor`. A central `Palette.cs` (colour tokens, one source for code and shaders) and `Config.cs` (all constants in metres and seconds). Each beat is a generator class `Beats/BeatNN_Name.cs` with `Build(Transform parent)`, so beats can be built by separate agents and tuned independently. The path is a smooth curve defined in code (`PathDef`). Everything is built at start behind a black screen (up to about 25 s is fine); **no generation work inside frames**; warm up all materials, shader variants and VFX during loading so there are no hitches in the walk; no per-frame heap allocations in the update loop.

**Event director and determinism.** Catastrophe events are functions of the **path position** (a trigger starts an event when the player passes a distance) and of their **own clocks** (an event runs for its designed duration regardless of the player's speed). All randomness is seeded; the same run looks the same. Because the cinematic mode moves at the Shift speed, the pacing of Part 10 is defined for that speed: if the player is slower, events must still look right (they run on their clocks and the camera is never harmed).

**Large worlds.** Anything larger than about 5 km from the origin needs a **floating origin** (re-centre the world around the player when it moves more than about 1 km) or camera-relative rendering; far backdrops (sky, distant mountains, distant storms) are placed at a fixed large distance and scaled for parallax. Jitter or shimmer at distance is a defect.

**Rendering baseline** (set early, then judge by eye; details in Part 10.0):
- HDR buffers, **fixed exposure** (no auto-exposure, except where Part 10 says otherwise), camera dithering on to avoid banding in fog and sky gradients, film grain off by default (video compression smears noise). Anti-aliasing: pick by eye; avoid ghosting trails on VFX and particles.
- Repeated objects with GPU instancing; distance fades or LOD cross-fade instead of popping; no sub-pixel thin geometry (wires and ropes at least 4 cm and softened by shader); no coplanar faces at distance; fog colour equal to the far backdrop colour; near plane small.
- Prevent defects instead of testing for them. Invisible bounds so the camera can never leave the allowed corridor or clip into geometry.
- Target: 60 fps average at 1440p on the RTX 5070 in the final profile with no hitch longer than 50 ms during the walk. If it falls short, reduce the single most expensive item.

**Audio (everything procedural).** A small DSP library in plain C# (oscillators, filtered and coloured noise, envelopes, LFOs, band-pass and low-pass filters, simple reverb or feedback delay, a distance model with **speed-of-sound delay** where the design needs it) driven by pure functions of progress and time. Play it through `AudioSource`s that use `OnAudioFilterRead` or `AudioClip.Create` buffers generated at load. A **master soft limiter on the AudioListener's object** (ceiling -1 dBFS). Never allocate in the audio thread. Offline check: render the sound beat by beat to WAV with a small tool and analyse peak (below -1 dBFS), clicks, DC offset and RMS curve with `python -I`.

# PART 8 — PLAYER EXPERIENCE, CINEMATIC MODE AND RECORDING

- **Controls:** mouse look (captured cursor, no smoothing lag, Y not inverted), `W` / `S` move along the path (the vehicle game has its own rule in Part 10), **`Shift` = brisk speed**, **`C` toggles the cinematic mode**, `Esc` pause overlay (Resume, Quit), `F11` fullscreen. Gamepad is optional and low priority. Use the Input System package.
- **Speeds.** The default speed, the Shift speed and the path length are given in Part 10.2. **The cinematic mode moves along the path at exactly the Shift speed** (the same number as a player who holds Shift in manual mode, not slower, not faster), with only a short ease at the start (about 0.5 s) and at the very end. It does not slow down at reveals: the drama is made by the camera, light and sound, not by changing speed.
- **Cinematic mode (`C`, or `--cinematic`):** the camera is handled like a film camera operator: **critically damped look targets** that follow the **gaze cues** you define per beat (where to look and when: first the establishing view, then the detail, then the reveal), FOV changes eased over many seconds, a tiny handheld sway (at most 0.3 degrees, never roll the horizon), and the player's mouse movement is ignored except that `C` hands control back within 1 second (eased) and `Esc` pauses. The same visuals and post-processing as the manual mode, so the footage can be recorded in either mode. The cinematic mode is how the user records the video, and it is also your test harness: invest in its gaze choreography as much as in the world. A small, thin, fading line "Cinematic mode" appears for 2 seconds when it is toggled (never in a harness capture).
- **Movement:** the player follows the path with a small free drift inside a safety corridor (radius per beat in Part 10), full free look (limits in Part 10), smooth acceleration (about 0.3 s). FOV 68 to 78 degrees, changed only slowly and eased. No jump, no crouch. A little breathing sway is welcome; never roll the camera.
- **Start:** from black, fade in over about 2.5 s with the title (thin, centred, gone after about 4 s). A one-line controls hint for about 6 s, which disappears at the first input and never shows in cinematic mode.
- **End:** reaching the goal starts the ending described in Part 10, then one quiet line of text, then black with Esc available.
- **Harness flags** (user arguments after `--`): `--cinematic`, `--start-at <seconds>` and `--end-at <seconds>` (jump straight to a point of the path **at the cinematic speed's clock**, with all event clocks consistent for that point, and stop there), `--capture-every <seconds>` (save a PNG of the window to `QA/shots/` at that interval of game time), `--profile test|final`, `--quit-at-end`, `--perf` (average fps, 1 % low fps, worst frame time, video memory to `QA/perf.txt`), `--flash-log` (write the per-frame mean luminance to `QA/flash.csv`). # PART 10 — GAME SPECIFICATION: ZERO

Units: metres, seconds. Fictional rural place, no real names. The world is empty: **no people, no bodies, no animals harmed** (birds are frozen mid-flight, unharmed and alive). Time is almost stopped; everything physical is shown in the first instant of the impact, with **dream logic** (air blast, thermal flash, ground shock and ejecta all exist at once, at a compressed, walkable scale; do not explain the physics to the player).

## 10.0 Project state and pipeline setup (do this first, Phase 2)
This project was created from the URP Blank template: Unity 6000.6.0f1, **URP 17.6.0**, Input System 1.20.0. `Assets/Settings` holds the PC and Mobile pipeline assets and renderers, `DefaultVolumeProfile.asset` and `UniversalRenderPipelineGlobalSettings.asset`; `Assets/Scenes/SampleScene.unity` is the template scene (replace it; delete the template tutorial content). Verify this yourself and record any difference in `DEVELOPMENT.md`. Configure from editor scripts: Forward+ renderer, HDR on, camera dithering on, depth texture and **opaque texture on** (for the refractive shock cone), SRP Batcher on, one main directional light (the sun) with stable cascades; the PC asset is the one in use. Custom renderer features and fullscreen passes must use the **Render Graph** API of this version (check `Library\PackageCache\com.unity.render-pipelines.universal@17.6.0`). Add `com.unity.visualeffectgraph` (17.6.x) for particles if it installs cleanly. URP has no volumetric fog: build the atmosphere from shader fog functions shared by all custom shaders and, if needed, a fullscreen depth-haze renderer feature.

## 10.1 Concept and look
**A walk through one frozen second.** An asteroid about 2 km long (a dream-scaled stand-in for a 10 km body) is touching the Earth. Time has almost stopped: the world runs at about 1/1500 of its speed. The player walks 4.6 km along a farm road toward ground zero, in real time and alone, through the zones of the impact in order: calm, the glowing line in the sky, the shadow, the frozen shock front, a museum of suspended destruction, fire and rolling earth, the ring wall of the crater, and finally the underside of the asteroid itself, with a single tiny tree under its tip. At the end **time comes back for one second**: sound, light, white, silence.

Mood: **numbness and the sublime**. Where game 1 is dread in daylight and game 2 is panic in the dark, this is cold, silent, impossibly beautiful stillness that is more frightening because nothing moves except you. The viewer knows time will resume and walks toward it anyway.

**Look: stylised high-key.** A bleached, bright, clean, graphic world: a white-blue sky, big simple forms, smooth gradients, crisp silhouettes, strong atmospheric perspective in at least five depth layers, almost no photographic texture noise; the plasma is the only intensely saturated, hot element and it **bleaches everything near it**. Shadows are cool deep blue. Not photorealistic, not low-poly: the clarity of a high-end illustrated art game. The sun and the plasma cast **two different shadows** (a signature visual of this game).

**Palette tokens** (`Palette.cs`, sRGB): SKY_WHITE `#f2f7ff`, SKY_BLUE `#bcd4f2`, HAZE `#dfe8f2`, WHEAT `#d8c48a`, FIELD_GREEN `#9fb27a`, ROAD_GREY `#8e9096`, DUST_TAN `#c9b089`, SHADOW_BLUE `#2d3b5c`, SHADOW_DEEP `#161d33`, PLASMA_WHITE `#fff7e6`, PLASMA_GOLD `#ffd27a`, PLASMA_ORANGE `#ff8a3c`, FIRE_RED `#e0401c`, ROCK_DARK `#2a2420`, MOLTEN `#ffb04a`. Grade: lifted blacks, bright airy mids, soft bloom on the plasma; no film grain.

**Camera:** FOV about 70 (cinematic 66 to 78, eased; wider only at the last reveal). Free look limits: none except staying inside the world. No hands, no body.

## 10.2 Path, speeds, hero effects and budgets
**Speeds.** Default 4 m/s, **Shift 7 m/s, cinematic mode = 7 m/s** (a purposeful, long stride; this world is dream-scaled so that 11 minutes cross all the zones). Path length about **4 620 m** (7 m/s x about 660 s): a farm road, then fields, then a cut up the ring wall, then the crater floor to ground zero at the end (distance to ground zero d = 4 620 minus chainage). Safety corridor radius 6 m on the road, 12 m in the fields and the crater. Human-scale references in every wide shot: the road and its markings, fence posts, poles, hay bales, a mailbox, wheat stems, birds, planks, a tractor, a car (always empty), the lone tree.

**Frozen time system (the soul of the game).** A single `WorldTime` with a world rate of about 0.0007 that **every world animation, VFX playback rate, shader time (`_WorldTime` global) and audio layer obeys**. Nothing is truly static: the frozen world has micro-motion (grass trembling, dust drifting, flames licking in extreme slow motion, debris turning a few degrees per minute), so it feels like held breath, not a mannequin display. The player, the camera and the footsteps run in real time. In the last 1.2 seconds the rate ramps from 0.0007 to 1 over about 0.4 s (see the ending).

**Hero effects (build and judge each in isolation first, Phase 3). Acceptance bars are by eye, in the test profile at 1280x720:**
1. **The asteroid.** A procedural body about 2 km long and 1.4 km wide (domain-warped noise displacement on a dense icosphere, ridges, craterlets, fracture planes, a hot molten underside with glowing cracks), seen in the shade of its own plasma. It must read as a **mountain-scale rock with surface detail at every distance** and with a changing silhouette as the player walks, never as a glowing sphere. Its tip is about 120 m above the ground at ground zero. Plasma sheath: layered emissive shells and VFX (white core, gold, orange edge, streaming filaments), lighting the ground and the players' surroundings.
2. **The trail and the bow shock.** A luminous, twisting column from the object up to the top of the sky (fading with height; slim at the top, thick near the body), and a **glass-like bell-shaped shock cone** around the object that refracts the world behind it (sample the opaque texture) with a thin bright edge. In the zone near the front the shock appears as a transparent, rippled wall that distorts the world as the camera passes through it.
3. **Double shadows and the second light.** The sun plus the plasma light: the plasma as a large additional light (spot or point with shadows within a limited range) so that objects cast two shadows of different colour and sharpness; if URP's additional-light shadows prove too costly or unstable, use a projected fake shadow pass near the player. The double shadow on the road in beats 1 and 2 is a money shot.
4. **The museum of destruction** (beats 3 to 5). Procedural, instanced debris sets (planks, shingles, leaves, soil clods, glass shards, water droplets from a sprinkler, feathers) in a radius of about 150 m around the player (seeded placement, streamed with the player), density following the zone; large frozen set pieces: a barn bursting apart plank by plank (about 300 pieces hanging with relative displacement), poles snapped mid-fall, trees bent flat in a common direction, a line of fence posts leaning like dominoes, a tractor and a car lifted and tilted, a water tower mid-collapse, **a wind turbine buckling in the middle of its tower** (about 100 m tall: the best scale object), a flock of birds with spread wings at many heights. Cap visible instances at about 200 000 in the final profile (100 000 in test), with LOD or distance fades and no huge single draw call.
5. **Ground and crater.** Fields flattened, then scorched, then **liquefied ground frozen in giant rolling waves** up to about 40 m high with glowing cracks, then the **ring wall** (about 100 m tall and 600 m thick, like a frozen ocean wave of rock) that the path climbs through a cut, then the crater bowl with a glass-like molten crust, glowing cracks and a **molten dome rising to meet the asteroid's tip**. Heightfield terrain or tiled meshes with shader displacement; no collision needed beyond a ground height function.
6. **Fire, smoke and ejecta.** VFX Graph at the world rate: frozen walls of fire in the fields, trees as torches, thin smoke columns, **thousands of ejecta arcs** (rocks with smoke trails hanging across the sky, a few house- to tower-sized blocks above the path), a plasma curtain in the bowl falling as vertical streaks. At most about 300 000 live particles in the final profile (150 000 in test).
7. **Large distances.** A **floating origin** (re-centre the world when the player moves more than about 1 km), a camera far plane that covers the sky objects, far backdrops at fixed distance. Jitter, shimmer or z-fighting at distance is an S1 defect. Prove it with a capture from the far end of the path.
8. **Stylised atmosphere.** Shared fog and aerial-perspective functions in every shader; at least five readable depth layers in every wide shot.

Budgets: final profile 6 GB of VRAM, test profile 4 GB (Part 4B). URP is lighter than HDRP; do not spend the headroom on cost: spend it on instance counts and particle quality.

## 10.3 Sound bible (all procedural, Part 7; two layers)
**The real-time layer** (the only living thing): the player's footsteps in a crisp, close, slightly too loud mix (road gravel, dry grass, tilled soil, plank debris, glassy crust, each with its own character), a very quiet high ring (like tinnitus) that creeps up over the game. **The world layer** is time-stretched: slow, pitched-down, muffled sound that arrives as long swells, a deep sub-bass pressure throb of about 0.5 Hz, creaking that lasts ten seconds per creak, wind that is a swell every 20 seconds, a metallic sustained groan from the object, and a distant, slowed roar. It deepens and thickens zone by zone; in beat 1 almost silence. **Crossing the shock front** is a sound event: the muffled world layer snaps to a close, heavy pressure sound for a moment. The ending: the world layer and the ring both cut out, one breath of total silence, then time resumes (below). Master limiter at -1 dBFS; the resume is prepared by that silence and ramps in over at least 0.3 s.

**Ending (beat 7).** When the player is within about 5 m of the lone tree the world rate ramps to 1 over about 0.4 s for about 1.2 s: the flame, the debris and the plasma move at full speed, the sound returns with a rush, the tip touches the molten dome, and the screen goes to white **with a ramp of at least 150 ms** (one single event: no flicker), holds for about 1.5 s, then total silence with a faint ring, a slow fade to black, and the closing line (one short line in thin type of your choice, such as a time stamp `T + 0.000 s`).

## 10.4 Beats (times at the cinematic speed 7 m/s; each ends with a "must read"; tension values in brackets)
**Beat 1 — An ordinary day (0:00-1:30, chainage 0-630 m) [3, uncanny].** A farm road through wheat in bright afternoon light, a fence, poles, a distant barn, a row of wind turbines. Only the player's footsteps; the world is nearly silent. A flock of birds hangs frozen over a field; dust motes hang in the air; the wheat is bent in a held gust. Far on the horizon: a thin vertical white line and a second, dimmer light. The player's shadow has a faint twin. *Gaze cues:* the road, the birds, the line in the sky, the double shadow on the road, forward. *Must read:* beauty, calm, and the first wrongness: nothing moves. **Stomach-drop:** realising that the birds are not moving.

**Beat 2 — The line (1:30-3:00, chainage 630-1 260 m) [5].** The line thickens into a luminous column from the horizon to the zenith; at its base a blindingly bright elongated shape wrapped in orange: the object. A glassy bell of shock stands around it. The grass ahead lies flat; a pale dust ring on the horizon. The double shadow becomes obvious: the plasma's shadow is sharper and warmer. The hum deepens. **Money shot M1 (awe):** the column, the double shadow on the road and the frozen flock, in one composition.

**Beat 3 — The shadow (3:00-4:30, chainage 1 260-1 890 m) [7].** A sharp line of dark sweeps across the fields: the player enters the object's shadow. Overhead the dark rock mass with glowing ridges lit from below looms like a ceiling; the air shimmers; birds hang thick at the edge of the shadow. The player's own shadow disappears into the dark. *Must read:* an object the size of a mountain range in the sky, indifferent. **Money shot M2:** the shadow boundary on the ground racing toward the player under the frozen birds.

**Beat 4 — The front (4:30-6:30, chainage 1 890-2 730 m) [8].** The shock front: a rippled transparent wall that distorts the world as the camera passes through it in about a second; on the other side the museum of destruction: flattened trees, poles frozen mid-fall, shingles and planks hanging in the air at every height, the barn bursting apart, the buckling turbine ahead above the fields, the tilted tractor and car. *Gaze cues:* the front as it approaches, a look back at the calm world just left (it is already small and far), then along the road through the hanging debris to the turbine. *Must read:* every object is caught in the middle of being destroyed; a thousand things hang at human scale around a giant at the end of the road. **Money shot M3:** a long axial view along the road through hanging planks to the buckling turbine. **Stomach-drop:** passing through the front and the sound turning heavy.

**Beat 5 — Fire and earth (6:30-8:30, chainage 2 730-3 570 m) [9].** The fields are on fire in frozen walls of flame; trees are torches; ahead the ground rolls in giant waves frozen mid-roll with glowing cracks. Overhead thousands of ejecta arcs hang across the sky, trailing smoke, with a few huge blocks (the player walks under one, its shadow cold on the path). *Must read:* the ground itself behaves like water; the sky is full of falling mountains. **Money shot M4:** the ejecta arcs across the whole sky with a small burning tree in the foreground.

**Beat 6 — The rim (8:30-10:00, chainage 3 570-4 200 m) [9].** The path climbs through a cut in the ring wall, a frozen wave of rock 100 m tall. At the crest the **crater bowl** opens: a glowing, glass-crusted basin, the molten dome in its centre rising to the asteroid's tip, and the asteroid's underside now filling more than 120 degrees of the sky above, with a plasma curtain falling. *Gaze cues:* the cut ahead (compression), then at the crest a slow sweep from the bowl up to the underside. *Must read:* the thing is far larger than the crater; there is no sky left. **Money shot M5 (megalophobia peak):** the crest vista.

**Beat 7 — Under it (10:00-11:00, chainage 4 200-4 620 m) [10, then release].** Down into the bowl across the glowing crust under a roof of rock; the plasma rain hangs in vertical streaks; the air is white-hot at the edges of the view; at the centre, on a small unmelted knoll, **one tiny tree**, perfectly calm. As the player reaches it, time resumes for 1.2 s (see the ending): white, silence, ring, black, the closing line. *Must read:* smallness against the ceiling of the world; the last calm thing; the return of time.

## 10.5 Research topics and cinematic references (Part 5)
Real: Chicxulub, Tunguska, Chelyabinsk 2013 (the light, the delayed shock wave, the broken windows), Meteor Crater, the *Earth Impact Effects* model for zones of damage (thermal flash, air blast, ground shock, ejecta, ring wave), atmospheric entry plasma and bow shocks, crater formation (the transient crater, central uplift and the ring wave), the **Trinity rapatronic photographs and Harold Edgerton's high-speed photography** (the look of the first instant, the milk-drop coronet), Schlieren and shock visualisation. Films to study (as written breakdowns): *Armageddon*, *Deep Impact*, *Knowing*, *Melancholia* (slow, silent awe), *Greenland*, *Don't Look Up*, *Interstellar* (scale and silence), and the **frozen-moment language**: *The Matrix* (bullet time), *X-Men: Days of Future Past* (the Quicksilver kitchen scene), *Inception* (the suspended cafe explosion), *Tenet* (time as a place). Questions for the cinematic bible: how do these films make a frozen scene look alive, how do they place the camera relative to suspended debris for depth, how do they use sound in a stopped world, how do they light an object that is both the light source and the danger.

## 10.6 Reveal discipline and priorities
Never show the biggest thing early: the object is a point and a line in beat 1, a bright shape in beat 2, a ceiling in beat 3, a sky in beat 6. **Stillness is the effect: make the micro-motion exquisite.** Priorities when time is short: (1) the frozen-time system and the asteroid, (2) the museum of destruction with the turbine and the barn, (3) the shadow and the shock front, (4) sound (the two layers, the silence, the resume), (5) the crater and the rim, (6) fire and ejecta, (7) everything else.
